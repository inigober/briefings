# News Briefing — 22 September 2026

Health systems — Madrid transplant labs, Germany's private-insurance split, African clinics after USAID — set the frame, with a jury date for Spain's prime minister's wife.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Spanish doctors are building transplants the body does not reject**

A team at Madrid's Hospital La Paz is teaching a recipient's immune system to treat a donor organ as its own — the "holy grail" of transplant medicine — and Spain's national transplant agency, the ONT, says the method could end lifelong immunosuppression. ([eldiario.es][1])

💡 If it scales, the constraint on transplant patients stops being the drug cocktail and becomes the organ supply.

* **Begoña Gómez has been sent to a jury trial**

Investigating judge Juan Carlos Peinado has committed the Spanish prime minister's wife to a jury on embezzlement and influence-peddling over her work at Madrid's Complutense University, which puts the alleged loss at €113,000. ([La Vanguardia][2])

* **The state will pay for a new Madrid home for Francesca Thyssen's collection**

Spain will fund a new building for the 365 works Francesca Thyssen-Bornemisza, the baron's daughter, has donated and loaned; she says the museum will not carry her name and that her TBA21 foundation and the pinacoteca will run it. ([EL PAÍS][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Merz is opening a fight with private health insurance**

Chancellor Friedrich Merz has questioned Germany's dual public/private health-insurance split, the system that lets higher earners leave the public funds for private cover; Handelsblatt asks whether this is a typical Merz stumble or a real turn. ([Handelsblatt][4])

💡 Touching that split is a coalition and voter test, not a technical tweak.

* **Planet Labs will start building satellites in Berlin**

US earth-observation firm Planet Labs plans a satellite production site in the German capital, pitching itself as a reliable supplier for European customers while keeping its headquarters in the United States. ([Handelsblatt][5])

* **Merkel's official portrait for the Chancellors' gallery is not red**

Angela Merkel is at the Bundestag to open an art show and talk about her new official portrait for the Kanzlergalerie — and about democracy — including why the painting is not red. ([Tagesspiegel][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Auto-industry workers took their job fight to Berlin**

Staff from Germany's car industry demonstrated in the capital over employment, a local show of a national squeeze in the sector that still anchors a large slice of German manufacturing. ([rbb24][7])

* **6,600 homes lost power on election night**

A technical fault blacked out parts of western Berlin on Sunday evening, leaving about 6,600 households without electricity until crews restored supply. ([Berliner Zeitung][8])

* **Leyla Yenirce on making art from violence**

The Berlin-based Kurdish artist talks about identity, a deliberately joyful morning routine, and turning something horrible into work that can be looked at. ([The Berliner][9])

## World 🌐

* **African healthcare is straining after the US aid pullout**

A new Accra Reset report says the Trump administration's retreat from USAID and other health programmes — stacked on Covid-era damage and higher living costs — has put care in Africa and the wider Global South "under growing strain," and that governments there should take more control of their own health development. ([The Guardian][10])

💡 The shock is not only fewer clinics; it is who sets the agenda once Washington leaves.

* **China's power-grid stocks are missing the AI boom**

Shares in the companies that build China's electricity infrastructure are lagging even as data-centre and semiconductor demand for power climbs — a market signal that the bottleneck may be the grid, not the models. ([Nikkei Asia][11])

💡 Yesterday's sodium-ion race was about cells; this is about whether the wires can feed the compute.

* **A UN expert says US boat strikes may be crimes against humanity**

A UN human-rights-council rapporteur finds "reasonable grounds to believe" that US attacks on alleged drug boats in the Pacific and Caribbean — more than 230 dead since September 2025 — meet that threshold. ([The Guardian][12])
