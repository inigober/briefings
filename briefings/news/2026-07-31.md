# News Briefing — 31 July 2026

Ceuta's largest border surge since 2021, a Supreme Court ruling on swim-back returns, and a Franco-Italian Schengen row frame a day when Brandenburg heat and wildfires test Berlin's hinterland and global markets weigh AI spending against China's slowing factories.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **More than 20,000 people cross into Ceuta in largest entry since 2021 crisis**

eldiario.es reports that local officials counted over 20,000 arrivals on Thursday as young Moroccans and Algerians scaled the breakwater or swam the strait, many citing economic desperation at home. Nine bodies were recovered from the sea as overwhelmed Guardia Civil and emergency crews struggled to process the influx. ([eldiario.es][1])

* **Supreme Court bars "hot returns," fuelling surge that forces army deployment**

La Vanguardia says a 8 July ruling that swim-back expulsions at the border are unlawful removed Madrid's main deterrent, helping explain why thousands now treat Ceuta as reachable once in the water. The judgment turns a technical asylum procedure into a live operational crisis as Pedro Sánchez prepares a Friday visit. ([La Vanguardia][2])

💡 When courts void instant pushbacks without a replacement pipeline, deterrence collapses faster than reception capacity can be legislated — a pattern now visible at Europe's most unequal land border.

* **Ceuta crisis strains Spain–Morocco ties as intelligence doubts Rabat's "accident"**

EL PAÍS reports Spanish services do not believe Morocco lost control of the frontier simply because of Throne Day celebrations, raising questions about whether the kingdom tolerated the breach to pressure Madrid on other dossiers. The diplomatic suspicion complicates Sánchez's bid to manage the surge while preserving counter-terror cooperation. ([EL PAÍS][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **China's factory and services mood cools at start of second half**

Tagesspiegel cites new PMI-style surveys showing industrial, services and construction sentiment weakening simultaneously as property stress and weak domestic demand weigh on the world's second-largest economy. For Germany's export machine, a simultaneous slowdown across Chinese sectors threatens orders just as Berlin debates how to offset US tariff shocks. ([Tagesspiegel][4])

* **DAX investors face six corporate flashpoints in earnings season**

Handelsblatt lists today's market calendar — from Apple and Amazon results to euro-zone GDP and US payrolls — as the German benchmark holds steady mid-season. The piece frames Frankfurt as a transmission belt for global tech capex and macro surprises rather than a domestic growth story. ([Handelsblatt][5])

* **Slovenian lawyer mobilises European clubs against FIFA's private-investment World Cup plan**

Tagesspiegel describes how a Ljubljana attorney is coordinating resistance to Gianni Infantino's proposed $25bn investor vehicle, arguing it would cede tournament governance to US banks and Gulf sovereign funds. The fight tests whether UEFA members can still block FIFA commercial experiments after the collapsed Super League precedent. ([Tagesspiegel][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Thunderstorms follow 38°C heat as Berlin–Brandenburg faces fourth summer scorcher**

rbb24 says Thursday temperatures topped 35°C across the capital region before evening storms uprooted trees in the Prignitz district north of Berlin. The pattern keeps pressure on hospitals, transport and wildfire crews already stretched by a week of field blazes in surrounding Brandenburg. ([rbb24][7])

* **Federal court to rule whether forwarding private WhatsApp chats can justify dismissal**

Berliner Zeitung reports the Bundesgerichtshof is reviewing a case where an employer sacked a worker who shared colleagues' messages with a union, after lower courts split on whether GDPR covers private group chats. The outcome will set a workplace privacy standard for millions of Germans who mix personal and professional messaging. ([Berliner Zeitung][8])

* **Tesla rehires first 1,000 workers at Grünheide plant as EV demand rebounds**

Tagesspiegel says the US carmaker has begun the first phase of a hiring wave at its Brandenburg factory east of Berlin, citing improved electric-vehicle orders after earlier layoffs. The partial reversal matters for a site that became a symbol of Germany's contested auto transition and local labour politics. ([Tagesspiegel][9])

## World 🌐

* **Cameroon's 93-year-old Paul Biya unseen for record 53 days, fuelling succession anxiety**

The Guardian reports Paul Biya has not spoken publicly since leaving for a Swiss hotel on 7 June, even though discussing the president's health is legally sensitive at home. The vacuum at the head of one of Africa's longest-ruling regimes raises stability questions across the francophone Sahel. ([The Guardian][10])

* **Asian equities rebound and yen jumps on suspected intervention**

The Financial Times says Tokyo shares recovered after days of AI-linked losses while the yen strengthened from 160 per dollar, suggesting authorities may have sold dollars to cap currency weakness. The move highlights how chip-cycle volatility and FX policy are now intertwined for export Asia. ([Financial Times][11])

* **Atlantic essay warns AI labs are reverting to "move fast and break things" culture**

The Atlantic argues frontier-model companies are shipping products faster than they can secure infrastructure, citing recent breaches and reckless agent deployments. The piece frames sloppiness as a structural risk as Washington debates export controls and liability rules. ([The Atlantic][12])
