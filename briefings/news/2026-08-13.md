# News Briefing — 13 August 2026

The morning after Spain's peninsular eclipse, Madrid's Ceuta headcount, German antimatter logistics and a Mexican accountability breakthrough share the day with Berlin's pre-election street politics and an African crop-pest discovery.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Government says up to 5,000 Ceuta migrants could be accommodated**

Madrid estimates roughly 2,500 unaccompanied minors and another 2,500 potential asylum seekers remain in the North African exclave after July's mass border crossing — the first consolidated reception figure since the crisis overwhelmed local services. The tally sets expectations for how many people Spain may need to shelter or return as courts and EU partners debate responsibility-sharing. ([EL PAÍS][1])

* **Priests, shop staff and thousands of workers kept working through totality**

While millions left offices to watch Spain's first peninsular total eclipse since 1905, EL PAÍS profiles a parish priest celebrating Mass, Gran Vía retail workers and other employees who could not abandon posts during the 80-second darkness — highlighting how Spain's service economy still runs on schedules that rarely bend for collective spectacle. ([EL PAÍS][2])

* **Twenty-five Santander residents chose a book talk over the sky**

At the Feria del Libro Viejo, a small group attended a Regino Mateo lecture that coincided with totality rather than joining crowds on the beach — a deliberate contraprogramming moment in a country that treated Tuesday as a national appointment with astronomy. ([eldiario.es][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Physicists plan to drive a suitcase of antimatter across Germany**

Two particle researchers are designing a road convoy to move antimatter — a substance whose half-gram would match the Nagasaki bomb's yield — from a production site to an experiment, forcing regulators to treat the world's most explosive cargo like any other hazardous freight. The project tests whether Europe can sustain cutting-edge physics without relying on airborne shipments that airlines increasingly refuse. ([Tagesspiegel][4])

💡 If antimatter logistics become routine, Germany keeps a foothold in fundamental research; if transport rules stall, experiments migrate to countries willing to bear the security bill.

* **Why Ukrainian Antonov freighters were parked at Leipzig when a drone struck**

Frankfurter Allgemeine explains that NATO has used Ukraine's Soviet-era cargo jets for outsized military transports, making Leipzig/Halle — a hub for heavy freight — a logical parking spot that became a target when an explosive-laden drone was found beside a parked Antonov last week. The detail reframes a Saxony police case as part of the war's spillover into civilian German infrastructure. ([Frankfurter Allgemeine][5])

* **Deutsche Bahn equips station staff with body cameras**

After rolling out bodycams for onboard crews, the state-owned operator will arm platform and ticket-hall employees with wearable cameras, citing rising assaults on frontline staff. The expansion follows months of political pressure after attacks on conductors across Berlin and Brandenburg and signals that rail safety policy now treats stations as conflict zones, not just trains. ([Tagesspiegel][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Prenzlauer Berg residents fear new bridge will become a party hotspot**

Neighbours of the rebuilt Schönfließer Bridge warn that wide pavements and river views will invite round-the-clock drinking like Kreuzberg's Admiralbrücke, citing noise, litter and public urination as construction nears completion. The dispute turns a routine transport project into a test of whether Berlin can build gathering spaces without reproducing the outdoor-party conflicts that define other inner-city bridges. ([Tagesspiegel][7])

* **SPD mayoral hopeful Krach campaigns harder than rivals in polling slump**

Spitzenkandidat Steffen Krach has spent weeks touring nearly every Berlin district while more popular opponents stay selective, a grind Tagesspiegel portrays as either desperation or the only path left for a Social Democrat trailing in surveys before September's Abgeordnetenhaus vote. The contrast matters because Berlin's fragmented race may be decided by visibility as much as programme. ([Tagesspiegel][8])

* **Brandenburg finance minister's draft budget still has major holes**

Ahead of a September cabinet presentation, rbb reports that Brandenburg's 2027–28 spending plan contains unresolved gaps in education, infrastructure and municipal transfers — a fiscal headache for the state wrapping Berlin on three sides. Shortfalls in the capital's hinterland often foreshadow fights over housing subsidies, regional rail and energy-transition costs that Berlin's next coalition will inherit. ([rbb24][9])

## World 🌐

* **Soil fungus offers natural weapon against Africa's armyworm pest**

South African scientist Letodi Luki Mathulwe traced fields of dead caterpillars coated in white fungus to a soil-borne pathogen that kills the African armyworm — a crop pest whose march-like swarms devastate maize across east and southern Africa. The discovery could give smallholders a biological alternative to costly pesticides if field trials confirm the fungus can be deployed at scale. ([The Guardian][10])

* **Former Guerrero governor charged over 2014 disappearance of 43 students**

Mexican prosecutors have formally indicted Ángel Aguirre, ex-governor of the southern state where 43 teacher trainees vanished in 2014, on forced-disappearance and obstruction charges including an alleged order to destroy surveillance footage. The case, a symbol of cartel violence and official impunity, gains new momentum weeks after Aguirre's arrest revived one of Latin America's most notorious human-rights mysteries. ([The Guardian][11])

* **Wealth managers cut fees to court AI's paper millionaires**

Banks and advisers are discounting services for employees at OpenAI, Anthropic and other frontier labs sitting on multimillion-dollar equity stakes that are still illiquid, the Financial Times reports — a sign that Silicon Valley's AI hiring boom is reshaping private-banking competition even before shares trade publicly. ([Financial Times][12])
