# News Briefing — 22 June 2026

Spaniards tell pollsters they no longer trust their courts to be impartial, Germany confronts how digital workplaces and inflated job references distort labour markets, and Colombia elects a hard-right president by the narrowest margin in its history — today's edition tracks institutional legitimacy under strain from the courtroom to the ballot box.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Majority of Spaniards say courts do not act impartially**

elDiario.es reports on an Ateneo del Dato survey finding that 47.8% of citizens believe judges and prosecutors do not treat left and right equally, while 31.8% say courts are harsher on the left and only 16% see bias against the right. The poll lands amid the Begoña Gómez prosecution and wider debates over judicial independence. ([eldiario.es][1])

💡 Perceived partisan justice is becoming a baseline political assumption — not a fringe complaint — with implications for whether corruption probes are read as law or as warfare.

🧩 If nearly half the electorate discounts court outcomes in advance, parliamentary reform of judicial appointments becomes harder to separate from partisan score-settling.

* **Valencia court opens probe into riot police assault on teacher during education strike**

elDiario.es reports that an investigating judge in Valencia has opened proceedings after a teacher filed a complaint alleging a riot officer struck her from behind during protests outside the regional education ministry. Police filings defend the intervention and accuse the victim of encouraging road occupations. ([eldiario.es][2])

💡 The case turns a sectoral pay dispute into a criminal-procedure test of how force is documented when teachers and riot units collide.

🧩 Valencia's education strikes already strained the Mazón government's crisis management; a upheld assault finding would deepen the region's policing accountability fights.

* **Spain rebounds at World Cup with commanding win over Saudi Arabia**

EL PAÍS reports that La Roja routed Saudi Arabia after a sluggish opening draw with Cape Verde, with Lamine Yamal scoring Spain's first goal of the tournament and Mikel Oyarzabal adding a brace. Luis de la Fuente's side looked sharper in press and transition, easing immediate fears about group-stage elimination. ([EL PAÍS][3])

💡 A routine group-stage correction matters because Spain's squad depth and age profile are being marketed as a generational handover — early stumbles invite tactical second-guessing.

🧩 World Cup performance still doubles as soft-power theatre for a government juggling corruption probes and budget survival.

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Labour courts inflate reference grades as employers fear litigation**

Die Zeit reports that German employers increasingly award glowing Arbeitszeugnisse even to underperforming staff because neutral wording is read as coded criticism and triggers costly dismissal suits. Labour lawyers say the "secret language" of references has warped into a risk-management tool that misleads future hirers. ([Die Zeit][4])

💡 Germany's famed worker protections now produce systematically dishonest hiring signals — a market failure created by procedural caution.

🧩 HR departments may turn to structured probation data and skills tests as written references lose informational value.

* **Labour lawyers spell out when bosses may read work messenger chats**

Handelsblatt's employment-law column explains that employers can monitor company messaging platforms only within strict Works Constitution Act limits — typically with works-council agreements and narrow operational purposes. Casual lunch-planning and colleague gossip remain off-limits even on corporate accounts. ([Handelsblatt][5])

💡 Remote-work normalisation is pushing chat surveillance into the same legal grey zone once occupied by email scanning and keystroke logging.

🧩 Works councils that fail to negotiate clear messenger rules now risk either unchecked monitoring or de facto bans that slow coordination.

* **Ryanair extends O'Leary through 2032 with options worth hundreds of millions**

Handelsblatt reports that Michael O'Leary has renewed his contract as Ryanair chief executive until 2032, with share-option packages that could reach nine-figure euro values if targets are met. The deal locks in the architect of Europe's ultra-low-cost model as rivals grapple with Boeing delivery delays and sustainability levies. ([Handelsblatt][6])

💡 Long-dated CEO packages at discount carriers signal confidence that fare wars and ancillary revenue — not fleet glamour — will dominate European aviation for another decade.

🧩 German airports and Lufthansa-group hubs must price around a Ryanair still led by its most aggressive cost disciplinarian well into the 2030s.

## Berlin 🏙️

* **National politicians join Berlin's expropriation debate five years after referendum**

Tagesspiegel reports that the 2021 Berlin vote to socialize large landlords is still legally stalled — yet Bavaria and other Länder are now arguing about whether similar expropriation models could spread nationwide. Housing economists say the capital's unresolved campaign keeps feeding a federal conversation Merz's coalition would prefer to contain. ([Tagesspiegel][7])

💡 Berlin's housing politics no longer stay local: a non-binding referendum is shaping how conservative states frame property rights.

🧩 Until courts rule on compensation mechanics, Vergesellschaftung remains a symbolic fault line that blocks more mundane rent-registry and construction reforms.

* **Mitte district pilots simpler permits for open-air cultural events**

rbb24 reports that Berlin-Mitte is testing streamlined approval paths for outdoor concerts and street festivals after organisers complained that post-pandemic bureaucracy made spontaneous cultural programming uneconomic. The pilot coincides with this weekend's Fête de la Musique, when hundreds of performances fill parks and courtyards. ([rbb24][8])

💡 Open-air culture has become an administrative stress test: cities want night-life vibrancy but permit backlogs push organisers toward unlicensed formats.

🧩 If Mitte's lighter rules reduce illegal events without safety incidents, other boroughs may copy a model that trades upfront scrutiny for post-hoc enforcement.

* **Police investigate antisemitic assault on passers-by in Charlottenburg**

rbb24 reports that officers arrested a man suspected of insulting and attacking pedestrians on Uhlandstraße in Berlin-Charlottenburg, with investigators treating the incident as ideologically motivated. The case adds to a sensitive summer security calendar that includes Veterans Day closures and Pride events. ([rbb24][9])

💡 Street-level hate incidents force Berlin to calibrate visible policing without turning every weekend into a sealed government quarter.

🧩 Memorial and festival seasons concentrate crowds where a single assault can dominate national debate about urban safety and minority protection.

## World 🌐

* **Colombia elects ultraconservative Abelardo de la Espriella president by razor-thin margin**

EL PAÍS reports that preliminary counts give De la Espriella roughly 49.7% against 48.7% for leftist Iván Cepeda — about 250,000 votes — in the closest presidential runoff in Colombian history. Outgoing president Gustavo Petro urged calm and said no winner should be proclaimed before official scrutiny concludes. ([EL PAÍS][10])

💡 The material development is the result: Colombia pivots from Petro's peace-process project to a security-first conservative who campaigned against "social incendiarism."

🧩 A contested count in a polarised society could reopen debates over paramilitary legacies that yesterday's runoff coverage already surfaced.

* **Bolivia declares state of emergency as roadblocks paralyse the country**

The Guardian reports that President Luis Arce deployed soldiers and bulldozers to clear barricades after more than six weeks of protests by farmers, Indigenous groups and coca growers against his conservative-aligned government. Unions have choked highways with rubble and debris, stifling food and fuel deliveries. ([The Guardian][11])

💡 Bolivia shows how Latin American fiscal crises are now fought through infrastructure siege rather than parliamentary no-confidence votes alone.

🧩 Military clearance of domestic blockades risks civilian casualties that could trigger wider Andean solidarity protests.

* **Chinese appliance brands seize share across Southeast Asia**

Nikkei Asia reports that Haier, Midea and other Chinese manufacturers are expanding distribution and local assembly across ASEAN markets once dominated by Japanese and Korean incumbents. Price competition and aggressive retail partnerships are displacing legacy brands in air conditioners, refrigerators and small electronics. ([Nikkei Asia][12])

💡 Southeast Asia is becoming a live testing ground for whether Chinese consumer-hardware champions can export dominance beyond their home market.

🧩 Japanese and Korean suppliers face a second front in the consumer-appliance war even as semiconductor tool sales to China already weaken.
