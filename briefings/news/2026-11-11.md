# News Briefing — 11 November 2026

Spain's housing-driven general strike, Germany's slide in climate rankings and Asia's AI-fuelled boom, with its pay tensions, all turn on one question: who captures the gains, and who pays the costs, of an economy reorganised around scarce assets.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Spain's unions began their first major general strike in 14 years, a protest over housing costs and stagnant wages called days before the 29 November election**

CC OO and UGT, the two biggest union confederations, expect "massive" but regionally uneven turnout, while PP, Vox and Junts accuse them of acting as an arm of the government. ([EL PAÍS][1])

* **Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez is doubling the number of cabinet meetings during the election campaign, holding extraordinary sessions on the first two campaign Fridays to pass more social measures**

The government has not said whether a planned pardon for former attorney general Álvaro García Ortiz will come before or after the vote, when it would be in caretaker mode. ([eldiario.es][2])

* **A study by consultancies KSNET and Daleph estimates that a decent life in Spain's big metropolitan areas requires a gross salary of €1,645 to €2,061 a month**

Single-parent households with one child need €2,196 to €2,949 because everything depends on one wage, a gap that helps explain the housing anger behind today's strike. ([eldiario.es][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Germany has slipped to the middle of a new international climate-protection ranking, with experts chiding in particular its planned expansion of natural-gas projects**

The ranking, published as the UN climate conference opens in Turkey, shows renewables expanding worldwide while Berlin loses ground on the leading group. ([Handelsblatt][4])

💡 A country that built its reputation on the Energiewende (its shift to renewables) is now being marked down for locking in new gas infrastructure.

* **Finance minister and Vice-Chancellor Lars Klingbeil is under fire over tax-class reform, with a Handelsblatt commentary arguing that the debate over cutting married couples' tax splitting distracts from a bigger omission**

The column argues that the dispute over tax classes III and V, which shape couples' monthly take-home pay, is the wrong question, and that a different failure by the SPD minister deserves more anger. ([Handelsblatt][5])

* **A study of around 80,000 people with diabetes finds that stopping weight-loss injections is linked to a markedly higher risk of heart attack, stroke and death**

Weight and blood sugar barely explain the increase, and specialists are cautious about what the finding means for patients. ([Tagesspiegel][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Four controversial Left Party politicians are among those negotiating interior policy in Berlin's coalition talks between the SPD, Greens and Linke**

Three of them rejected the exploratory paper on tackling antisemitism, and one once advocated for men from the Barbakh clan, which makes the interior portfolio the most sensitive part of the talks. ([Tagesspiegel][7])

* **Fourteen city-owned companies, including the Vivantes hospitals and the BSR refuse service, launched a poster campaign against violence towards their staff**

Mayor Kai Wegner is calling for tougher penalties, and the firms say physical attacks, sometimes with weapons, are no longer rare. ([rbb24][8])

* **Berlin's domestic intelligence agency (Verfassungsschutz) is refusing to say whether it is monitoring the Berlin branch of the far-right AfD**

The silence matters because a formal "suspected case" designation would allow surveillance of the party in the capital. ([rbb24][9])

## World 🌐

* **Japan's planned AI data centre in Akita prefecture is expected to anchor a regional technology hub and lift local tax revenue**

Nikkei Asia presents the project as a model for regions seeking AI investment and the tax revenue that comes with it. ([Nikkei Asia][10])

* **Foreign Policy argues that Democrats, who won the US midterms, now hold a mandate to tackle corruption, starting with investigations and subpoenas before moving to campaign-finance reform**

The argument frames the new Democratic majority's first task as oversight of the Trump administration rather than legislation. ([Foreign Policy][11])

* **Asian stocks rose for a third day on enthusiasm for artificial intelligence, while regional bonds fell after hotter-than-expected US inflation strengthened the case for the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates**

Equity investors are looking past higher borrowing costs, a combination that leaves markets exposed if the AI trade stumbles. ([Bloomberg][12])

💡 Equities rising while bond yields climb is the reverse of the usual pairing, which makes the rally depend heavily on AI earnings.
