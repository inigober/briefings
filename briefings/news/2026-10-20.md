# News Briefing — 20 October 2026

A Vox pair's tax-court loss and an Oxfam housing-poverty count sit beside German mortgages at 2009 highs, Berlin's left opening formal talks, and a Kenyan Ebola cluster the US is no longer built to contain.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **A Madrid court says Espinosa de los Monteros cheated the tax office on a luxury attic**

The Madrid high court has thrown out four of five appeals by Iván Espinosa de los Monteros, Vox's former Congress spokesman, and Rocío Monasterio, his partner and the party's former Madrid leader, against tax-agency fines for a false invoice and other devices used on Spain's most expensive penthouse deal. ([eldiario.es][1])

💡 The ticket that campaigns against housing rules now has a luxury-attic tax-fraud judgment on the file.

* **Housing costs push 770,000 workers below the poverty line**

Oxfam Intermón finds that once rent or a mortgage is taken out of pay, Spain's working-poor rate is 15.5% — about three million people — and that housing costs alone drop 770,000 of them into poverty. ([EL PAÍS][2])

* **The lesson from the 2024 DANA is the alert, not only the rain**

Comparing this week's *dana* Adam with the 2024 Valencia floods, EL PAÍS argues that mass-alert messages and prevention — still incomplete — are what change the death count. ([EL PAÍS][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **German mortgages are as expensive as they have been since 2009**

Handelsblatt's "Trumpflation" note says President Trump's Iran war has pushed inflation and, with it, borrowing costs, so German home loans are at their dearest since 2009 — a freeze for buyers and for housebuilding. ([Handelsblatt][4])

💡 The war premium is now in the *Bauzins*, not only in the oil price.

* **Statutory doctors say a new slot-reporting rule will lengthen waits**

The Kassenärztliche Bundesvereinigung, which represents Germany's public-insurance doctors, says a plan to make practices declare a fixed number of free appointments — instead of choosing how many to offer — will not shorten queues; the *Terminnot* will get worse. ([Tagesspiegel][5])

💡 Publishing the empty slots is being sold as access; the doctors' body says it is a rationing display.

* **The €99.99 family rail ticket is coming back**

Deutsche Bahn is restoring a family return ticket at €99.99 for up to five people, keeping saver fares from €6.99 and cutting commuter monthly cards by 25%. ([Berliner Zeitung][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **The SPD and Greens have said yes to formal talks**

A day after this briefing flagged the Monday vote, Berlin's SPD state board and the Greens have agreed to open coalition negotiations with Die Linke; the Left still has to endorse that at a party congress at the end of October. ([rbb24][7])

* **The Greens will not let Graf and Jarasch pick the Senat**

After criticism of co-leaders Werner Graf and Bettina Jarasch, and a poor election result, the Berlin Greens are handing the choice of possible government members to a four-person party commission. ([Tagesspiegel][8])

* **Alba beat the cup holders with a short bench**

Alba Berlin, the city's basketball club, knocked title-holders Bamberg out of the BBL-Pokal 83–71 despite a thin roster. ([rbb24][9])

## World 🌐

* **Kenya's first Ebola cluster is a US health-retreat story**

An eighth person in Kenya has tested positive for the Bundibugyo strain — the country's first cluster — as an outbreak that has already killed more than 4,300 in the region meets a United States that has cut WHO funding, banned some travellers, and is building a quarantine centre. ([The Guardian][10])

💡 The American instrument on offer is a camp and a ban, not a WHO budget.

* **Investors are buying default insurance on the AI boom**

Trading in credit-default swaps on big-tech names has surged as investors hedge against the infrastructure boom coming apart — protection priced on last week's dumped neocloud bonds. ([Financial Times][11])

* **Publishers fighting AI scrapers have hit the Wayback Machine**

An Atlantic essay argues that blocking the Internet Archive does not stop model training and does delete the public memory of the web — a collateral-damage story from the same copyright fight. ([The Atlantic][12])
