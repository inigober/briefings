# News Briefing — 12 September 2026

Documented night returns of children from Ceuta, a Hamburg bid for Israel's ZIM line, and a US cap on Hormuz escorts set the frame as Berlin's Art Week opens in a ruined Charité lecture hall.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Minors say they were pushed back from Ceuta without the legal process**

Three children who reached Spain's North African enclave in late July now speak from Morocco: they told officers they were minors and were still returned at night, outside the Extranjería procedure — the foreigners law that is supposed to govern removals — and eldiario.es has videos of Guardia Civil operations at the fence. ([eldiario.es][1])

💡 Yesterday's story was an open-air queue at the shelters; this is a documented return practice, not another street scene.

* **The Civil Guard has suspended a lieutenant colonel tied to the Vive Madrid case**

The officer, posted at the force's immigration-and-borders headquarters, is under Audiencia Nacional investigation for laundering money from a sexual-exploitation ring allegedly run by his partner; the corps has now taken him off duty for six months after his passport was already withdrawn. ([eldiario.es][2])

* **Kent Nagano will open his run with Spain's national orchestra on Mahler's Second**

The Japanese-American conductor — one of the names still booked by big opera houses — debuts this month with the Orquesta Nacional de España and, in passing, says a collaboration with Rosalía would be a dream. ([EL PAÍS][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Hapag-Lloyd is still chasing ZIM after an Israeli veto**

Germany's largest container line is in talks on a takeover of ZIM, the Israeli carrier, worth about €3.6 billion, and is treating Jerusalem's objection as a hurdle to work through rather than a stop. ([Handelsblatt][4])

💡 A Hamburg shipping bid is now also a test of how far Israeli politics can block a European fleet deal.

* **The CDU in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern is polling near the five-percent line**

The centre-right party that leads the federal government stands at about 7% ahead of the 20 September Landtag vote in the northeastern state — close enough to the threshold that keeps a party out of the legislature. ([Handelsblatt][5])

* **German hunting rules still allow stray cats to be shot**

Schleswig-Holstein recorded almost 2,600 cat kills in a hunting year; under national game law a cat can be shot from as little as 200 metres from a house, a looser regime than Italy's and closer to Australia's pest framing. ([Berliner Zeitung][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Art Week is using a ruined Charité lecture hall as a place to lie down**

The city's annual contemporary-art festival is staging concerts and writing sessions in a Hörsaalruine — a wrecked teaching theatre — at the university hospital, with the invitation to listen and work from the floor rather than the foyer. ([Tagesspiegel][7])

* **Insiders say Steffen Krach pushed hard on a Lower Saxony clinic-site sale**

The SPD's lead candidate for the 20 September Abgeordnetenhaus election is already under a Hannover bribery probe; Tagesspiegel's latest reporting reconstructs his role in selling a hospital plot, which prosecutors are also examining. ([Tagesspiegel][8])

* **The Eisbären have their first Champions Hockey League win**

Berlin's DEL club — the professional ice-hockey side that plays at the Mercedes-Benz Arena — beat Graz in the continental group stage. ([rbb24][9])

## World 🌐

* **Washington has cut Hormuz tanker escorts to two daily slots**

After a rise in Iranian attacks, the US will only provide air-defence cover for merchant ships in the Strait of Hormuz — the Gulf chokepoint that carries a large share of seaborne oil — at two set times a day. ([Financial Times][10])

💡 Rationing escorts is a capacity limit being written into the timetable, not just another incident report.

* **Japan wants the UN's new 'true-scale' map revised over the Kurils**

The General Assembly adopted a projection that enlarges Africa and shrinks Europe and North America — and draws the disputed Kuril islands as Russian; Tokyo has asked for a change, and Washington has also objected. ([The Guardian][11])

* **An AI-made pandemic is not the outbreak to plan for**

The Atlantic's health brief argues that a virus designed by a model is still a distant scenario; ordinary evolution, it says, remains the likelier source of the next one. ([The Atlantic][12])
