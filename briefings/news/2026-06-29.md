# News Briefing — 29 June 2026

A widening Plus Ultra probe and fresh Valencia metro bribery claims test Spain's judicial credibility, Germany's industrial job losses meet a labour-law fight over who pays for training, Berlin searches for heat fixes beyond record thermometers, and AI-driven power deals in America plus an Nvidia-backed factory in Indonesia collide with Putin's first admission that Ukrainian drones are denting Russian fuel supplies — today's edition tracks how corruption memory, workplace rules and energy infrastructure are reshaping politics faster than institutions adapt.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **PP allegedly tried to bribe Valencia metro victims' families to stay silent**

eldiario.es reports that Juan Cotino, a former Aznar-era police chief and Camps conseller, offered public-sector jobs to relatives of people killed in the 2006 Valencia metro crash if they refrained from joining legal proceedings — part of a broader PP strategy to silence families ahead of the twentieth anniversary on 3 July, when 43 deaths remain Europe's worst underground-rail disaster. Victims' associations say the offers were systematic; the PP has not commented on the specific allegations. ([eldiario.es][1])

💡 The material development is archival evidence surfacing now: two decades on, new testimony suggests corruption extended beyond engineering failures to active suppression of bereaved families.

🧩 If courts reopen compensation claims, Feijóo's national anti-corruption pitch collides with a Valencia wound that still defines regional conservative politics.

* **Podemos asks judge to summon police inspector harassed for investigating PP**

eldiario.es reports that Podemos has requested judge Santiago Pedraz call inspector Manuel Morocho as a witness in the guerra sucia case, after Morocho told the Kitchen trial he was exiled to the PP's internal political brigade and handed the PISA dossier on Pablo Iglesias. Morocho says harassment followed his refusal to drop probes into conservative networks — linking the mask-contract trial to parallel dirty-war operations against Podemos and Catalonia. ([eldiario.es][2])

💡 Kitchen is no longer a standalone corruption file: Morocho's testimony ties mask kickbacks to a broader pattern of police weaponisation against opposition parties.

🧩 If Pedraz grants the request, PP leaders may face simultaneous scrutiny over judicial manipulation, Catalonia operations and Podemos surveillance — widening the scandal's political blast radius.

* **Quirón takeover of Madrid central lab sparks clash with displaced workers**

eldiario.es reports that Quirón Salud, which won a €120m concession to run the Laboratorio Clínico Central serving six Madrid public hospitals, told existing staff on their first day under new management that they could not continue — triggering protests and union complaints. Workers say they clocked in, signed attendance sheets and were immediately dismissed; the regional government has paid Quirón hospitals roughly double budgeted sums over four years. ([eldiario.es][3])

💡 Madrid's outsourcing model is colliding with labour reality: a private operator inheriting a public workforce cannot simply declare staff redundant on day one without political fallout.

🧩 Ayuso's health privatisation agenda faces a courtroom and parliamentary test over whether concessions protect service continuity or merely transfer margin to contractors.

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Handelsblatt warns industrial jobs are vanishing while Berlin looks away**

Handelsblatt's morning briefing argues that structural breaks at VW, Mercedes and Bosch are eliminating manufacturing posts faster than policymakers acknowledge — yet the federal government is preoccupied with other agendas. The analysis contends a cognitive error prevents Berlin from treating deindustrialisation as an active crisis requiring industrial strategy, not merely cyclical adjustment. ([Handelsblatt][4])

💡 Germany's debate shifts from whether factories shrink to whether the coalition even recognises the shrinkage as a governing priority.

🧩 Merz's reform package may land on a workforce that no longer believes automotive rescue plans will arrive before the next round of plant closures.

* **Labour-law column clarifies when employers must fund worker training**

Handelsblatt reports that German employment law obliges firms to pay for training they mandate, while voluntary upskilling sits in a grey zone workers often misunderstand. Legal experts say employees sent to compulsory courses are entitled to fees and time costs, but self-initiated qualifications may receive only partial support — a distinction gaining importance as AI and green-tech transitions force mid-career reskilling. ([Handelsblatt][5])

💡 Skills policy is becoming a wage-and-benefits negotiation: who pays for adaptation determines whether transition costs fall on workers or capital.

🧩 With the coalition debating pension and contribution reforms, training-cost rules may become the next flashpoint between unions demanding employer-funded reskilling and firms seeking state vouchers.

* **WHO signals German cruise-ship Hantavirus outbreak is nearing end**

Tagesspiegel reports that the World Health Organization says the Hantavirus cluster linked to a May cruise that alarmed German public-health authorities is winding down, with no new cases in recent weeks. The outbreak had triggered intense media scrutiny over rodent-borne disease on passenger vessels calling at Baltic ports. ([Tagesspiegel][6])

💡 The material development is official closure: Entwarnung lifts a summer travel anxiety that had put cruise operators and port health offices under microscope.

🧩 German regulators may still tighten embarkation screening after the scare — adding another compliance layer to an industry recovering from pandemic disruptions.

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Experts propose cooling fixes for Neukölln's Karl-Marx-Straße heat hotspot**

rbb24 reports that urban planners identify Karl-Marx-Straße as one of Berlin's hottest corridors, where dense paving and limited tree canopy amplify heat beyond neighbourhood averages — yet planting more street trees is not feasible along much of the strip. Specialists suggest alternative shading, reflective surfaces and water-misting infrastructure as interim measures while longer redevelopment proceeds. ([rbb24][7])

💡 Berlin's heat policy is moving from thermometers to street-level design: some corridors cannot be fixed with generic tree-planting targets.

🧩 Senate climate adaptation budgets will face neighbourhood equity questions if wealthy districts green faster while commercial arteries like Karl-Marx-Straße rely on cheaper technical fixes.

* **Union Berlin faces squad gaps as new coach Lustrinelli takes charge**

rbb24 reports that with pre-season training imminent, Bundesliga club Union Berlin still has significant roster holes under new head coach Mauro Lustrinelli, prompting questions about whether sporting director Horst Heldt must act in the transfer market. The piece profiles two players central to the rebuild and the tactical fit Lustrinelli inherits from a turbulent relegation battle season. ([rbb24][8])

💡 Local football governance matters when a district club's European-era ambitions meet a thin squad and a rookie coach — stadium politics for tens of thousands of weekly attendees.

🧩 If Union enters the season under-staffed, the Alte Försterei's community-owned model faces pressure to spend in a market where Berlin rivals have deeper investor backing.

* **Berlin pentathlete Marvin Dogue wins overall World Cup title**

rbb24 reports that Marvin Dogue became the first German modern pentathlete to secure the overall World Cup series, capping a season of consistent fencing, swimming and riding performances. The Berlin-based athlete's victory raises profile for a sport rarely in capital headlines outside Olympic years. ([rbb24][9])

💡 A local athlete breaking a national drought in a niche Olympic discipline signals how Berlin's sports ecosystem produces champions outside football's shadow.

🧩 Dogue's result may bolster arguments for sustaining elite-training facilities in a city where municipal sports budgets compete with heat-adaptation and housing spending.

## World 🌐

* **AI data-centre demand fuels record $200bn US power-sector M&A wave**

Financial Times reports that American utilities and generators are pursuing a dealmaking blitz worth roughly $200bn as hyperscalers scramble to secure electricity for AI data centres — the largest power-sector consolidation wave on record. Buyers are paying premiums for firms with grid connections and gas or nuclear capacity near emerging compute clusters. ([Financial Times][10])

💡 AI competition is now a land-and-wires race: model labs need electrons before they need chips, redirecting capital into unglamorous power assets.

🧩 Regulators may face pressure to fast-track interconnection queues while climate advocates question whether gas-heavy deals lock in fossil dependence for decades.

* **Australia's Firmus announces Nvidia-backed 'AI factory' in Indonesia**

Nikkei Asia reports that Australian infrastructure firm Firmus will build an Nvidia-equipped AI data-centre campus in Indonesia, targeting Southeast Asian cloud demand and government digitalisation goals. The project positions Jakarta's orbit as a regional compute hub as neighbours compete for foreign tech investment amid Gulf energy uncertainty. ([Nikkei Asia][11])

💡 Compute infrastructure is migrating to archipelagos with cheaper land and permissive investment regimes — not only to traditional US or EU hubs.

🧩 ASEAN governments must balance data-sovereignty rules against the capital required to host frontier-model training clusters.

* **Putin admits Ukrainian drone strikes are causing Russian fuel shortages**

Financial Times reports that President Vladimir Putin acknowledged domestic fuel-supply problems for the first time, linking shortages to Ukrainian long-range drone attacks on refineries that have dented production. The admission follows months of Kyiv strikes deep inside Russia and contrasts with earlier official assurances that consumers would not feel supply pain. ([Financial Times][12])

💡 Kyiv's refinery campaign has moved from symbolic sabotage to a constraint Moscow can no longer deny — shifting war economics toward infrastructure attrition.

🧩 European capitals watching diesel markets must weigh whether sustained Russian fuel tightness raises export curbs or price spikes that rebound on EU buyers.
