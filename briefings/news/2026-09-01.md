# News Briefing — 1 September 2026

Spain's courts are being locked from the right, Germany is retiring the Post name, Berlin's apprentices cannot find a room, and Japan's 10-year yield has left the 1990s.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Two years of judicial appointments have locked conservative control of the top benches**

The Asociación Profesional de la Magistratura, the main conservative judges' association, has taken 59 of 158 senior posts — 37%, more than the other three associations combined — as the General Council of the Judiciary starts a term that must fill four seats on the Supreme Court chamber that tries ministers and other office-holders. ([eldiario.es][1])

💡 The next aforados cases will be heard by a bench whose recent promotions already lean one way.

* **Madrid has doubled Ceuta's adult places in a week — and ruled out moving people to the peninsula**

Since the government created a single command, temporary adult places in the enclave have gone from 1,800 to more than 3,300; Pedro Sánchez still declines to blame Morocco and is pairing the lock-in with a new aid package. ([eldiario.es][2])

* **Ceuta's chief prosecutor says there is almost one sexual assault a day**

Silvia Rojas says the city's courts and shelters are close to collapse, and that the only way to ease the load is to send unaccompanied minors to the peninsula — the transfer Madrid has just refused for adults. ([EL PAÍS][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Deutsche Post is dropping its German name for DHL**

The former state postal service bought the US logistics firm DHL after 2000 and then went global while keeping a German holding name; that name is now going, leaving the yellow brand as DHL. ([Handelsblatt][4])

💡 A privatised monopoly is finishing the job: the last German word on the letterbox disappears.

* **The AfD's useful allies are the ones who say they are not fans**

A Tagesspiegel investigation follows lobbyists and fellow-travellers outside the party who hide sympathy while making its agenda look ordinary — a firewall problem that is cultural, not just parliamentary. ([Tagesspiegel][5])

* **US visitors are remaking Germans' favourite holiday island**

A direct New York–Palma flight has lifted American tourist numbers on Mallorca and pulled more luxury hotels into the market, pushing up prices on the Balearic island German families have treated as a default. ([Tagesspiegel][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Tempelhof-Schöneberg may change hands at the town hall**

After the CDU's strong showing in the repeat vote, the race for district mayor is now a test of whether the Greens can hold the Rathaus against a centre-right challenge in one of the city's larger boroughs. ([Tagesspiegel][7])

* **Apprentices are the ones the housing market will not house**

1 September is when thousands of Azubis — trainees on Germany's dual vocational track — start work; in Berlin, low training pay and a tight rental market leave many of them without a room, even with the city's Azubiwerk hostels. ([rbb24][8])

* **Hertha has signed Gustaf Nilsson for a season**

The Swedish centre-forward, who played at this summer's World Cup, joins Hertha BSC in the 2. Bundesliga to fill the striking gap the club has carried since last season. ([rbb24][9])

## World 🌐

* **Japan's 10-year yield has hit 3% for the first time since 1996**

The benchmark government-bond rate, pinned near zero for a generation of cheap money, has now cleared a level last seen in the late 1990s; US Treasury secretary Scott Bessent is signalling that he expects the Bank of Japan to keep raising. ([Financial Times][10])

💡 For the world's largest creditor, 3% is not a blip — it is the price of ending the post-bubble rate era.

* **China is trying to make AI work in hospitals — and has not settled who pays**

A state-backed push is putting models into clinics faster than insurers or patients have agreed a bill, turning a technology race into a health-finance question. ([Foreign Policy][11])

* **A UN committee says states are legally obliged to consider slavery reparations**

The Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination argues that a 1965 convention, not the law of the slave-trade era, requires countries to weigh comprehensive measures for the transatlantic trade and its racial afterlife. ([The Guardian][12])
