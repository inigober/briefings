# News Briefing — 24 October 2026

Spain's campaign hardens and its courts press on citizenship, Germany's leaders face questions over the presidency and AI at work, and Russian money in Alaska, US pressure on Cuba and Indonesian populism show power tested abroad.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Feijóo fields hard-edged candidates to compete with Vox**

Ahead of the 29 November election, the People's Party (PP) leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo has named Cayetana Álvarez de Toledo as his number two on the lists and Xavier Albiol, who wants to treat housing access as a public-order issue, to head the Barcelona ticket. ([eldiario.es][1])

* **Consulates scramble to meet the Supreme Court's five-day ultimatum on the "grandchildren law"**

Spain's top court has again ordered the government to identify people naturalised under the *ley de nietos*, which grants citizenship to descendants of Civil War exiles, who proved exile descent, and staff describe it as "repairing a plane in flight" while the foreign ministry will not say how many offices have reported. ([EL PAÍS][2])

* **A Lorca play is cancelled in Castilla y León, and its authors report the regional government to prosecutors**

The company Proyecto 43-2 says the regional government, in which Vox sits, pulled its play *Federico* for "ideological" reasons and wants to vet the "ideology" of artistic proposals before subsidising them, which the authors call veiled censorship. ([eldiario.es][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Merz is under pressure at the CSU conference to say whom he wants as federal president**

The Christian Social Union (CSU), the Bavarian sister party of Merz's CDU, wants Ilse Aigner for the largely ceremonial presidency, and Chancellor Friedrich Merz is being pushed to settle his preference, possibly on the conference stage today. ([Handelsblatt][4])

* **AI is becoming a reason to quit, as some staff find the workload heavier and the career path unclear**

A dpa report says AI raises productivity but leaves some employees with more work and no visible future, so firms hoping to keep skilled workers will have to change how they deploy it. ([Tagesspiegel][5])

💡 The labour-market risk shifts from AI replacing workers to AI driving them out, which makes retention a cost for employers in a country short of skilled labour.

* **A large majority of Germans back globally binding rules for AI**

As governments debate worldwide rules for artificial intelligence, a new survey finds broad German support for them. ([Handelsblatt][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Berlin police see no surge in calls for school blockades before a nationwide protest day**

Pupils' discontent is also high in Berlin and a nationwide protest day is being mobilised, but the police say they have seen no increase in blockade appeals. ([Tagesspiegel][7])

* **Eisbären Berlin start weakly but still beat Augsburg**

The ice-hockey champions of the German league (DEL) won despite a poor opening. ([rbb24][8])

* **Merz visits Frankfurt (Oder) to see chip technology**

The chancellor toured a chip-research site in the Brandenburg border city, east of Berlin, in a visit to German semiconductor ambitions. ([rbb24][9])

## World 🌐

* **US executives quit a $25bn Alaska gas project over claims Russia's Novatek secretly controlled it**

The former chief executive of Polar Train LNG, a planned export venture in Alaska, says Russian gas group Novatek financed it and held the reins without Washington's approval. ([Financial Times][10])

💡 Sanctioned Russian capital inside a flagship US energy project shows how hard it is to wall off strategic supply chains from Moscow.

* **Rubio tells Cubans "now is the time" to push for change after Trump floats a naval intervention**

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio urged Cubans seeking political change to act, soon after President Donald Trump told a Florida rally that US warships returning from the Iran war could intervene in Cuba. ([The Guardian][11])

* **Indonesia's populist president Prabowo Subianto is losing his charm**

The Economist argues that the erratic policies of Indonesia's president are holding back South-East Asia's largest economy. ([The Economist][12])
