# News Briefing — 27 August 2026

Spanish courts are still billing Madrid's credit-guarantee era, Germany is hunting for brighter economic facts, Berlin's clubs and Left are taking stock, and AI packaging plus a Himalayan flood set the wider frame.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Spain's Supreme Court has confirmed almost €200,000 in Avalmadrid fines**

Avalmadrid, Madrid's regional loan-guarantee agency and a long-running PP-era supervision mess, has now had Banco de España fines of almost €200,000 on participating firms and board directors upheld by the Supreme Court in the first half of this year. ([eldiario.es][1])

💡 The credit-guarantee swamp is still throwing off penalties on a court calendar, not as a fresh scandal.

* **Ceuta's housing fight is now being used as a brake on returns**

The government says people will be returned faster once viable sites are agreed; Ceuta president Juan Jesús Vivas still refuses sports halls and has threatened to chain himself, so the enclave's reception deadlock is being treated as a lever on removals. ([EL PAÍS][2])

* **Mike Scott is unpacking Fisherman's Blues in Valencia**

Mike Scott, the Scottish leader of The Waterboys, is in Valencia with the record that made the band's name: a 1988 album assembled with 96 musicians, and a studio history he says includes parts he does not remember having played. ([EL PAÍS][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Handelsblatt thinks Germany looks better than the gloom — if you pick the right facts**

Chancellor Friedrich Merz wants a country that only looks forward; the paper's morning briefing says that pitch is too lyrical, but that a handful of economic facts do support a less bleak reading than the one now dominating German debate. ([Handelsblatt][4])

* **Brandenburg's health minister will back a sugar tax only if the money buys prevention**

A planned levy on sugary drinks has a supporter in Brandenburg health minister Wilke, who would tie introduction to using the revenue for prevention rather than treating it as a pure consumption tax. ([Tagesspiegel][5])

* **German law still splits child cyclists by age, not by how families actually ride**

Until they are 8, children on bikes belong on the pavement; they may then choose pavement or cycle path until the choice ends, after which ordinary carriageway rules apply — a map of the road code, not a new statute. ([Handelsblatt][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Berlin's clubs are surviving. They are not thriving.**

A new Clubcommission report, from the city's club-industry body, finds nightlife still standing after years of closures and cost shocks, but far from healthy — a sector being managed as a shrinking piece of urban infrastructure. ([The Berliner][7])

💡 Survival without recovery is an industrial diagnosis, not a vibe.

* **The Left's Elif Eralp wants a properly red city hall, and is leading on rents**

Die Linke, now Berlin's largest party by membership and narrowly ahead in polls, is sending Elif Eralp into the Abgeordnetenhaus race with an "embracing" campaign whose main fight is high rents rather than attack ads. ([rbb24][8])

* **A partial lunar eclipse is due before dawn on 28 August**

After this month's solar eclipse, a partial lunar eclipse is due in the early hours of Friday; anyone in Berlin who wants to see it has to get up while the sky is still dark. ([rbb24][9])

## World 🌐

* **A Taiwanese packager wants to put AI chips on panels first**

Powertech, a Taiwanese chip-packaging firm, is aiming for the world's first panel-level packaging line for AI processors in 2027 — a larger-format way of assembling chips that would ease a bottleneck foundries cannot fix on the wafer alone. ([Nikkei Asia][10])

💡 The next scarce input in the AI stack may be the box the chip goes in, not another fab.

* **A Himalayan flash flood has killed more than 160, with hundreds still missing**

A flash flood on Nepal's border with China has killed more than 160 people, leaving local residents, foreign tourists, workers and security personnel unaccounted for as rescuers work through mud and debris. ([Financial Times][11])

* **Ukraine's real drone weapon is the software that aims them**

Delta, Ukraine's battlefield-management system, stitches sensor data into a miles-wide "kill zone" for drones, a layer credited with helping to slow Russian advances to a near standstill. ([The New York Times][12])
