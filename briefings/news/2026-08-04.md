# News Briefing — 4 August 2026

Tobacco lobbyists blocking plain-packaging laws, Berlin's Kantstraße cycle-lane rollback, and a Silicon Valley split over Chinese AI models set today's frame as Germany's DAX hits records while carmakers stall and Pakistan's jailed opposition leader keeps shaping politics from prison.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Tobacco firms threatened multimillion-euro lawsuits over plain-packaging push**

Spain's Health Ministry has twice withdrawn generic-cigarette-packaging rules from draft laws since 2024 after tobacco manufacturers warned they would seek indemnities on the scale of the multi-billion-euro settlements Britain and Australia paid when similar measures passed. The stalled reform leaves one of Europe's largest smoking populations without the visual warnings public-health officials say cut youth uptake. ([EL PAÍS][1])

* **Influencer work is maturing from side hustle into a stable career path**

A growing cohort of Spanish content creators now list social-media influencing as their sole occupation, with some reporting monthly earnings of €20,000 while others still treat it as precarious gig work. The shift shows how platform economies are producing identifiable professional tiers — not just viral one-offs — as brands route more marketing spend through individual accounts. ([eldiario.es][2])

* **EU interior ministers meet on migration amid Ceuta fallout**

European Union home-affairs ministers gathered in Brussels as Italy and Spain traded accusations over border management in the wake of Ceuta's mass crossing, with Rome and Madrid publicly blaming each other on the eve of the session. The tense choreography underscores how national migration crises are now fought through EU institutional forums before policy shifts materialise. ([La Vanguardia][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **DAX hits record while premium carmakers BMW, Mercedes and VW lose ground**

Germany's benchmark equity index climbed to a new high even as the country's flagship automakers reported weakening orders and margins, exposing a widening gap between financial-market optimism and industrial performance. The divergence raises questions about whether Frankfurt's rally reflects domestic growth or simply capital flowing toward sectors less exposed to Chinese competition and EV transition costs. ([Handelsblatt][4])

* **Nine million crypto holders face tax uncertainty as Berlin weighs new rules**

Roughly nine million Germans own Bitcoin or other digital assets, but proposed tax changes and a prolonged price slump have left holders unsure how gains will be treated and whether to trade. The standoff matters because retail crypto exposure has grown from a niche bet into a mass household balance-sheet item that policymakers can no longer ignore. ([Handelsblatt][5])

* **Four people shot on Cologne street, some seriously injured**

Gunfire in northern Cologne on Monday evening wounded four people, several seriously, in an incident police are still investigating. The attack adds to a summer of public-security incidents across western German cities that have kept interior ministries under pressure ahead of autumn state elections. ([Tagesspiegel][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Antisemitic tirade targets Jewish culture ship on Wannsee**

A motorboat driver shouted Holocaust-denial slogans at the MS Goldberg, a Jewish cultural vessel hosting its final day on Berlin's Wannsee lake, prompting a police investigation for incitement to hatred. The incident on one of the capital's most symbolic waterways underscores how public antisemitism is resurfacing in everyday leisure spaces, not only at political rallies. ([Tagesspiegel][7])

* **Senate removes Kantstraße cycle lane after years of dispute**

Berlin's transport administration is dismantling a contested cycle path on Kantstraße in Charlottenburg, ending a years-long standoff over bus lanes, fire-service access and cycling infrastructure that had split local residents and advocacy groups. The rollback shows how coalition politics and legal challenges can reverse urban-mobility experiments even after lanes are painted and in daily use. ([rbb24][8])

* **Berlin antiquarians flooded with bulk orders suspected for AI training**

Second-hand bookshops across Berlin report a Canadian company placing unusually large orders for obscure titles, raising suspicions the volumes are being scanned to train artificial-intelligence models. Antiquarians warn the practice could drain rare print stocks and sits in a legal grey zone over copyright and fair use. ([rbb24][9])

## World 🌐

* **Imran Khan's influence endures three years after Pakistan jailed him**

Pakistan's former prime minister remains the gravitational centre of opposition politics despite serving a three-year sentence on corruption charges his supporters call politically motivated. The persistence of his movement — with rallies and online mobilisation continuing — shows how populist leaders can shape elections and coalition arithmetic from behind bars. ([Nikkei Asia][10])

* **China's abandoned methanol bet is paying off for industrial policy**

An old Ford-China methanol-fuel project that looked like a dead end has become a case study in why Beijing keeps multiple energy pathways alive rather than betting exclusively on battery electric vehicles. Foreign Policy argues the lesson for industrial planners elsewhere is that shelving a technology option too early can leave economies without alternatives when market conditions shift. ([Foreign Policy][11])

* **Silicon Valley splits over whether cheap Chinese AI models are a threat or opportunity**

Low-cost open-weight language models from Chinese labs are dividing US tech executives and policymakers over whether to embrace the efficiency gains or block them on national-security grounds. The debate mirrors earlier semiconductor and cloud disputes and could determine whether Washington tries to ring-fence AI markets the way it has restricted advanced chips. ([Rest of World][12])
