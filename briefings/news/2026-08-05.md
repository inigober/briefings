# News Briefing — 5 August 2026

Refrigerated morgues for Ceuta's dead, Beijing's retroactive offshore tax hunt, and Berlin glass facades killing migratory birds set today's frame as Madrid hospitals struggle with stranded foreign patients and Germany's energy minister bets on falling power prices.

## Spain 🇪🇸

* **Court slashes corrupt ex-judge's bid to jail journalist for 22 years**

Madrid's provincial court has dropped hate-crime charges and a profit motive aggravation from Salvador Alba Mesa's case against Carlos Sosa, editor of Canarias Ahora, cutting the former judge's maximum sentence demand from 22 years to a fraction of that after a four-year legal battle opened in 2022. The ruling narrows one of Spain's most contentious press-freedom fights, in which a disgraced magistrate tried to use criminal law against a reporter who exposed judicial corruption in the Canary Islands. ([eldiario.es][1])

* **Italy wants Brussels to lead EU talks with Morocco, not Madrid alone**

Italy's interior minister has proposed that the European Commission take charge of migration diplomacy with Rabat, arguing bilateral deals such as Spain's leave other member states dependent on Madrid's bilateral bargains. The pitch tests whether the post-Ceuta crisis will produce pooled EU bargaining power or deepen north-south splits over who sets Morocco policy. ([La Vanguardia][2])

* **Foreign patients languish in Madrid hospital emergency wards**

A Bolivian man with terminal cancer and a Peruvian teenager have spent days in the emergency unit at Madrid's Puerta de Hierro hospital without transfer to inpatient beds, in cases staff attribute to cost-saving pressure on cases involving migrants without local family networks to complain. The backlog illustrates how Spain's "national priority" rhetoric on migration collides with hospital triage when patients lack advocates. ([EL PAÍS][3])

## Germany 🇩🇪

* **Energy minister Reiche bets electricity prices will fall as reforms bite**

Economy minister Katherina Reiche says industrial power costs should decline over the medium term as grid-fee reforms and expanded renewable capacity take effect, even though manufacturers still rank German tariffs among Europe's highest. The forecast matters because high electricity prices remain the chief complaint of energy-intensive firms deciding whether to invest at home or relocate production. ([Handelsblatt][4])

* **Illegal hair-transplant clinics dupe thousands with fake doctors**

A Tagesspiegel investigation finds a shadow market of unlicensed hair-restoration clinics across Germany luring patients with cut-price offers while employing staff who are not registered physicians — in one case advertising a surgeon who did not exist. The scam exploits a cosmetic-surgery boom and weak enforcement at a time when medical tourism advertising targets young men online. ([Tagesspiegel][5])

* **ING Deutschland to launch own credit card before Christmas**

Germany's largest direct bank plans to issue its first proprietary credit card in time for the holiday shopping season, adding a missing piece to its digital-only retail offering as neobanks and Apple Pay erode traditional branch relationships. The move signals how even established low-cost banks are racing to own the full payments stack rather than rely on third-party card networks. ([Handelsblatt][6])

## Berlin 🏙️

* **Millions of birds die each year colliding with Berlin's glass buildings**

Berlin's NABU chapter estimates millions of migratory birds are killed annually when they strike untreated glass facades across the capital, a toll activists say could be cut with patterned glass or protective film that building owners rarely install. The figure turns an invisible urban-wildlife problem into a planning question as the city keeps approving glass-heavy developments. ([rbb24][7])

💡 Bird collisions are one of the few biodiversity metrics where a single municipal building code change could measurably cut deaths — yet retrofit mandates remain voluntary.

* **Berlin's dog registry still lacks enforcement teeth**

A year after the city pledged a mandatory canine database to trace dangerous animals, Berlin's register remains largely voluntary and under-used, according to the Tagesspiegel's morning briefing — leaving bite incidents hard to trace and owners unaccountable. The failure shows how locally popular pet politics often stalls on data infrastructure and police follow-through. ([Tagesspiegel][8])

* **Theatre collective laborgras stages "Joyful Failure" on embracing defeat**

Berlin experimental troupe laborgras opens a new work exploring how performers and audiences can treat failure as creative material rather than something to hide, part of the capital's fringe scene that tests ideas before they reach mainstream stages. The show adds to a summer of small-venue programming as larger institutions wind down for the holidays. ([The Berliner][9])

## World 🌐

* **China launches retroactive global hunt for decades of offshore tax**

Beijing has opened a campaign to pursue citizens' historical offshore assets and tighten future capital controls, the Financial Times reports, as fiscal pressure and slowing growth push the state to recover revenue from wealth parked abroad. The sweep signals that China's tax net is widening beyond domestic earners to diaspora capital that long operated in legal grey zones. ([Financial Times][10])

💡 Retroactive enforcement risks accelerating capital flight before new rules bite — the same dilemma that made India's 2016 demonetisation politically explosive.

* **Takeda seeks to add dengue shot to India's national vaccine programme**

Japanese drugmaker Takeda is lobbying New Delhi to include its dengue vaccine in India's routine immunisation schedule, a step that would give the shot mass-market scale in one of the disease's largest endemic zones. Success would test whether India's procurement agency will favour a foreign manufacturer over domestic rivals in a politically sensitive public-health category. ([Nikkei Asia][11])

* **Colombia swears in costumed anti-graft activist as senator**

Luis Carlos Rúa, who campaigned as the cartoon character "Elefante Blanco" to protest corruption, took his oath in a plush elephant costume as one of 102 senators seated in Bogotá this week after revealing his identity days before the election for safety reasons. The stunt-turned-mandate shows how performance protest can enter formal institutions when voters reward anti-establishment symbolism over traditional party machines. ([The Guardian][12])
